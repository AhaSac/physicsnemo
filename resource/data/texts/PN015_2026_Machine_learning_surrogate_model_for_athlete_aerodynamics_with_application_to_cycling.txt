                                 Abstract                                      023
 Aerodynamic drag dominates the resistive forces in many sports at             024
 racing speeds, and small athlete posture changes can produce prac-            025
 tically meaningful changes in performance. Standard approaches like           026
 field testing, wind-tunnel testing and computational fluid dynamics can       027
 be reliable, but time-consuming. Recent advances in scientific machine        028
 learning have enabled surrogate models that predict flow quantities at a      029
 fraction of the computational cost of a full computational fluid dynam-       030
 ics simulation. However, most applications currently focus on industrial      031
 geometries, such as cars or airplanes, where the surface is morphed to
                                                                               032
 generate large datasets for training. It remains unclear how well these
 methods transfer to athlete geometries where variability is dominated
                                                                               033
 by articulated pose changes rather than smooth shape morphing. In             034
 this work, a dataset for cyclist aerodynamics is generated by combining       035
 12 scanned athlete geometries with 20 postures per athlete. Simula-           036
 tions are performed for these 240 geometries, which are then used to          037
 train a state-of-the-art surrogate model. An investigation is made on         038
 the generalization of the model to an unseen geometry and the bal-            039
 ance between number of positions and number of unique geometries in           040
 the dataset. The surrogate reproduces the drag area with good agree-          041
 ment, with all predictions within ±10 % and a mean absolute percentage        042
 error of 2.69 %. The results also indicate that increasing the number of
                                                                               043
 unique athlete geometries improves performance more effectively than
 increasing the number of postures per athlete at fixed dataset size.
                                                                               044
 These results support the feasibility of scalable surrogate models for flow   045
                                                                               046

      1 Introduction
058
059   Aerodynamic drag is one of the key determinants of performance in high-
060
      speed sports, such as cycling and downhill skiing. Athlete posture has a direct
061
062   effect on drag and, consequently, on power demand at race speeds. This makes
063
064   athlete aerodynamics a central topic in sports engineering research.
065
          Field tests, wind-tunnel testing and computational fluid dynamics (CFD)
066
067   have been the dominant tools for investigating and improving athlete aerody-
068
069   namics [1, 2]. Field testing captures the athlete in a fully realistic operating
070
      environment, but knowing the exact boundary conditions is difficult and
071
072   environmental variability is hard to control. Wind tunnels can provide a con-
073
074   trolled and realistic environment, but are expensive and time-constrained.
075
      CFD can provide richer field information, but high-fidelity CFD remains
076
077   computationally demanding.
078
079       An emerging alternative is machine learning (ML) surrogate modeling,
080
      where a model is trained on a dataset of simulations to approximate the
081
082   CFD mapping from geometry and boundary conditions to aerodynamic quan-
083
084   tities of interest. Over the last years, deep learning surrogates have become
085
      a computationally efficient alternative in science and engineering [3–5]. CFD
086
087   surrogate modeling, however, offer additional challenges: industrial and high-
088
089   fidelity problems can involve large meshes, the number of simulation samples
090
      is typically limited, and the physics is strongly geometry-dependent [6].
091
092
                                            ML for athlete aerodynamics        3

   One of the earlier demonstrations that ML could support aerodynamic              093
                                                                                    094
workflows was using a custom shape and field parameterization coupled with          095
Gaussian-process regression[7]. This later led to several different approaches      096
                                                                                    097
for aerodynamics-oriented surrogate approaches. Several authors have used           098
                                                                                    099
grid-based encodings of geometry, for example signed distance fields (SDFs),        100
together with convolutional architectures such as 3D U-Nets [8, 9]. Another         101
                                                                                    102
class of methods learns directly on meshes or point clouds using graph or           103
                                                                                    104
message-passing formulations [10, 11]. In parallel, the scientific ML community     105
developed operator learning methods that aim to learn mesh-free mappings            106
                                                                                    107
between function spaces. Examples include DeepONets [12], neural operators          108
                                                                                    109
and Fourier neural operators (FNO) [13, 14], and geometry-aware exten-              110
sions that better accommodate complex domains [15]. These approaches have           111
                                                                                    112
accelerated progress in large-scale simulation surrogates in general, includ-       113
                                                                                    114
ing applications relevant to external aerodynamics [16]. However, a challenge       115
for both operator-learning and mesh-based methods is scalability to large,          116
                                                                                    117
high-fidelity geometries and fields. Many approaches require substantial down-      118
                                                                                    119
sampling of the underlying simulation mesh, which can degrade accuracy and          120
generalization, and can be particularly problematic when the flow features of       121
                                                                                    122
interest are localized or geometry-induced [6, 17, 18]. A further practical issue   123
                                                                                    124
is that many models implicitly depend on the spatial structure of the train-        125
ing discretization and can lose accuracy when evaluated on different point          126
                                                                                    127
samplings, for example uniform point clouds or alternative meshes [17].             128
                                                                                    129
   Recent architectures aim to improve on these limitations by separating           130
geometry encoding from field prediction, and by enabling decoding at arbitrary      131
                                                                                    132
query points. One example is DoMINO (Decomposable Multi-scale Iterative             133
                                                                                    134
                                                                                    135
                                                                                    136
                                                                                    137
                                                                                    138

139   Neural Operator), a point-cloud based surrogate that learns multi-scale geom-
140
141   etry representations, extracts local encodings, and predicts surface and volume
142   fields via dynamically constructed local computational stencils [17].
143
144        Although these technologies are promising, it is important to evaluate their
145
146   performance across different fields and disciplines. In this context, athlete
147   aerodynamics represents a distinct challenge. Rather than morphing a single
148
149   smooth surface family, such as in the DrivAer dataset [19, 20], athlete geome-
150
151   tries feature large pose variations with articulated limbs, self-occlusion, and
152   posture-dependent flow separation and wake dynamics.
153
154        In this work, a state-of-the-art surrogate model is trained on a large CFD
155
156   dataset for flow over cyclists, generated using the virtual skeleton method
157   [21] to efficiently produce posture variations. The motivation is not to replace
158
159   wind tunnel experiments or CFD simulations, but to enable new workflows
160
161   and additional applications by reducing inference time by orders of magnitude.
162   Concretely, the following questions are proposed: accepting the inherent uncer-
163
164   tainties and limitations of the underlying CFD model, can an ML surrogate
165
166   reproduce the CFD outputs with significantly reduced runtime, and does it
167   generalize to (i) new postures within the training distribution and (ii) athletes
168
169   not seen during training. The trade-off between the number of athlete geome-
170
171   tries and the number of postures per athlete is investigated, with the objective
172   of establishing practical guidance for dataset design in sports-aerodynamics
173
174   surrogate modeling.
175
176        The novel aspects of the presented methodology can be summarized as:
177
178   • Use of a virtual skeleton method to generate a large training dataset for flow
179
          over cyclists with systematic posture variability.
180
181   • Demonstration of the viability of modern scalable neural surrogate methods
182
183       for sports aerodynamics
184
                                              ML for athlete aerodynamics          5

• Investigation of generalization to both unseen postures and unseen athletes           185
                                                                                        186
  outside the training dataset.                                                         187
• Exploration of the balance between the number of athlete geometries and               188
                                                                                        189
  the number of postures per athlete when constructing training datasets for            190
                                                                                        191
  sports aerodynamics.                                                                  192

259   2.2 Computational fluid dynamics simulations
260
261   The CFD setup mirrored the configuration previously validated for ath-
262
263   lete–posture aerodynamics [25]. The simulations were run using the online
264
265   simulation platform AeroCloud (NablaFlow, Stavanger, Norway). This allowed
266   efficient generation and simulation of the large datasets using Amazon AWS
267
268   cloud infrastructure. The simulation software was OpenFOAM-v2212. Turbu-
269
270   lence was modeled with the k-omega SST closure, pressure–velocity coupling
271   used the SIMPLE algorithm, and second-order schemes were applied for spatial
272
273   discretization of all transported variables.
274
275
276
                                              ML for athlete aerodynamics         7

   The computational domain was sized to maintain a small blockage ratio               277
                                                                                       278
(≲ 3%), with the inlet placed 5 body lengths upstream and the outlet 10 body           279
lengths downstream. The model was centered laterally and vertically.                   280
                                                                                       281
   A uniform inflow speed of 15 m/s, representative of time–trial conditions           282
                                                                                       283
was prescribed, together with a free-stream turbulence intensity of 0.5 %. A           284
fixed static pressure was set at the outlet with zero–gradient conditions for          285
                                                                                       286
the remaining variables. Lateral and top boundaries employed slip (symmetry)           287
                                                                                       288
conditions. No–slip walls were imposed on the rider surfaces with smooth–wall          289
roughness parameters.                                                                  290
                                                                                       291
   Unstructured meshes were generated using snappyHexMesh with localized               292
                                                                                       293
refinement around the rider and in the near wake. Anisotropic prism layers             294
(typically 14–16 layers, growth factor ≤ 1.2) were extruded from the surface to        295
                                                                                       296
resolve the boundary layer. The first–cell height was chosen to obtain y + ≲ 2         297
                                                                                       298
over the entire body, with a first cell-height of 2 µm. The cell lengths outside the   299
prism layer near the rider were 4 mm, and the overall cell counts were around          300
                                                                                       301
10 million. Each case was advanced until residuals plateaued and integral forces       302
                                                                                       303
stabilized, and the running mean of the drag force reached a convergence level         304
of 1 × 10−4 .                                                                          305

2.3 Machine learning surrogate model                                                   308
                                                                                       309
                                                                                       310
The chosen ML surrogate model was the DoMINO architecture [17]. DoMINO
                                                                                       311
was developed for large-scale external aerodynamics problems and released              312
                                                                                       313
as open-source by NVIDIA (Santa Clara, United States) through the Physic-              314
                                                                                       315
sNeMo framework [26].
                                                                                       316
   In this model, the geometry is represented as a surface point cloud aug-            317
                                                                                       318
mented with auxiliary geometric descriptors. The outputs are predicted surface         319
                                                                                       320
quantities (pressure and wall shear stress) evaluated at a set of query points,
                                                                                       321
                                                                                       322
      8     ML for athlete aerodynamics

323   from which integral quantities such as drag can be computed using the same
324
325   post-processing operators as for the CFD data.
326       Aerodynamic loads on athletes depend on both global posture (torso angle,
327
328   head position, frontal area) and local interactions (arm/torso gaps, leg spacing,
329
330   shoulder and helmet regions) that can alter separation and wake development.
331   DoMINO captures this by combining a multiscale global encoding of the sur-
332
333   face point cloud with local neighborhood features computed around discrete
334
335   query points. For each query location, the model samples a local subregion,
336   applies dynamic point-convolution kernels to extract geometry-aware features,
337
338   and aggregates information from nearby points to predict the surface quantity
339
340   of interest. The prediction is refined iteratively over multiple steps, which helps
341   represent multiscale dependencies while maintaining a point-based formulation
342
343   suitable for articulated postures.
344
345       The DoMINO configuration for this study used only the surface mode, as
346   the primary objective was accurate prediction of drag via integrated surface
347
348   pressure and shear stress. The configuration of the model has many hyper-
349
350   parameters, that could potentially be tweaked for optimal performance. The
351   choice of hyperparameters aligns mainly with the default settings. A key
352
353   setting is the latent interpolation resolution, which controls the spatial gran-
354
355   ularity of the learned global representation used when decoding predictions
356   at query points. In the present configuration, the latent grid resolution was
357
358   set to 128 × 64 × 48, providing a relatively fine discretization of the domain
359
360   in the streamwise, lateral, and vertical directions. This choice represents a
361   practical trade-off. Higher latent resolutions can better preserve sharp spatial
362
363   variations and geometry-induced gradients that influence surface pressure and
364
365   wall shear stress, but increase memory footprint and training cost. Lower res-
366   olutions reduce cost but may over-smooth the latent representation. 200 000

507   4 Discussion
508
509
      The results demonstrate that a modern point-cloud neural-operator surrogate
510
511   developed for external aerodynamics can transfer effectively to flow over ath-
512
513   letes, despite the markedly different nature of geometric variability. In contrast
514
      to common industrial benchmarks that rely on smooth morphing of a base
515
516   surface, athlete posture variability is dominated by articulated limb motions.
517
518        A key practical contribution is the finding that the number of unique ath-
519
      lete geometries is more important than increasing the number of postures per
520
521   athlete. This suggests that the dominant generalization challenge is not only
522
523   the variation in poses for a fixed body, but the distribution shift introduced
524
      by different anthropometrics and local shape details. This conclusion is par-
525
526   ticularly relevant for sports engineering contexts where access to multiple elite
527
528   athletes is limited.
529
           Important limitations should be acknowledged. First, the CFD data are
530
531   based on a certain computational setup, which constrains the surrogate to the
532
533   uncertainties and biases of that modeling choice. The surrogate should there-
534
      fore be interpreted as a fast emulator of this CFD configuration, rather than
535
536   a general predictor of real-world performance. Second, the study only inves-
537
538   tigated prediction of surface quantities used for drag area prediction and did
539
      not attempt to reproduce full volume fields, which limits flow-physics inter-
540
541   pretability. Third, equipment and geometric assumptions are constrained, for
542
543   example the use of a single helmet model for all subjects, which may reduce
544
      robustness to other equipment choices. Finally, the dataset contains a limited
545
546   number of unique scanned subjects, which is sufficient to demonstrate the via-
547
548   bility of the approach, but not to claim broad population-level coverage. In
549
      particular, the model would be expected to only be applicable to time-trial
550
551   positions and not more upright road positions.
552
                                          ML for athlete aerodynamics       13

   These limitations motivate several directions for further work. Expanding      553
                                                                                  554
the number of unique athlete geometries is the most direct path to improv-        555
ing accuracy and robustness. This could include other positions in cycling, or    556
                                                                                  557
even other sports such as downhill skiing. Incorporating additional operating     558
                                                                                  559
conditions, such as yaw angles, would improve practical relevance. A system-      560
atic comparison with alternative scalable surrogate paradigms would also be       561
                                                                                  562
of interest.                                                                      563
                                                                                  564
   Despite these limitations, the approach appears to be viable, especially for   565
new application areas where fast results are more important than high accu-       566
                                                                                  567
racy. Combined with advances in pose estimation, these could, for instance,       568
                                                                                  569
include drag area overlays on television broadcasts, bike fitting applications,   570
and virtual wind tunnels.                                                         571

5 Conclusion                                                                      575
                                                                                  576
A surrogate model trained on a CFD dataset generated with a virtual skeleton      577
                                                                                  578
posture pipeline can predict cyclist drag area with good accuracy, including      579
generalization to geometries not used during training. With 240 simulations in    580
                                                                                  581
the dataset, the trained model reproduced drag area values within ±10 % for       582
                                                                                  583
all test cases and achieves a mean absolute percentage error of 2.69 %, while     584
also capturing the dominant surface pressure patterns with only localized dis-    585
                                                                                  586
crepancies in sensitive regions. A central practical finding is that increasing   587
                                                                                  588
the number of unique athlete geometries improves surrogate accuracy more          589
effectively than increasing the number of postures per athlete in the tested      590
                                                                                  591
regimes. This work demonstrates that modern scalable external-aerodynamics        592
                                                                                  593
surrogates can provide actionable, rapid posture evaluation for flow over ath-    594
letes, opening the door for new workflows and application areas for sports        595
                                                                                  596
aerodynamics.                                                                     597
